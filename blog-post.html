Vitrified or ceramic? Which tile belongs in which room

Water absorption, foot traffic and finish decide this, not price. Here is how we talk it through at the counter, room by room.

Most tile arguments are really one question asked badly: how much water will this tile meet, and how many feet will cross it? Answer those two and the choice between vitrified and ceramic makes itself. Price follows the answer; it does not lead it.

The one number worth learning

Water absorption is the percentage of its own weight a tile will take on when soaked. It is printed on the carton and it tells you more about the tile than the photograph on the front does. A dense body absorbs almost nothing, so it does not swell, it does not stain from underneath, and it does not come loose when the substrate is damp.

TileWater absorptionReads best as
Full-body vitrified Under 0.5% Floors throughout, wet areas, outdoors
Glazed vitrified (GVT) Under 0.5% Floors where the pattern matters more than the edge
Ceramic 3–10% Walls, light-traffic rooms, budget-led work

Full-body vitrified carries its colour the whole way through, so a chipped edge does not flash white. Glazed vitrified carries the same dense body but the pattern sits in a printed, glazed skin — far better looking, slightly less forgiving of a knock. Ceramic has a softer body and a glaze doing all the work, which is exactly right on a wall and wrong on a hallway floor.

Room by room

Living rooms and hallways

Vitrified, in the largest format the room can carry. Fewer grout lines read as a calmer floor, and the dense body takes a suitcase being dragged across it. Large-format needs a flat screed — if the floor is out, the tile will telegraph it.

Kitchens

Vitrified underfoot with a matt or lappato finish, never a high polish. Oil on a polished floor is the single most common slip complaint we hear. On the backsplash, ceramic is entirely sensible: it is a vertical surface meeting splashes, not standing water.

Bathrooms

Floor: vitrified, matt, with a slip rating you have actually looked at. Walls: ceramic or glazed vitrified, whichever gives you the finish you want. The waterproofing membrane under the tile matters more than either choice — a good tile over a bad membrane still ends in a damp ceiling downstairs.

Balconies, terraces and driveways

Full-body vitrified rated for outdoor use, laid to a fall so water leaves. Anything with a porous body will take on water, freeze-thaw or sun-bake, and lift. This is the one place where going a grade up is never regretted.

Ask for the PEI rating too.

Absorption tells you about water; PEI tells you about abrasion. PEI III is fine for a home, PEI IV and up for a shop, an entrance or anywhere shoes arrive from the street. A beautiful PEI II tile in a busy hallway will look tired in two years.

Finish changes the room more than the material

Two tiles with the same body and the same size can make completely different rooms. Polished bounces light and shows every footprint. Matt holds its look and hides dust. Lappato sits between the two, with a soft sheen that photographs well and still gives some grip. Carving and structured finishes add texture for outdoor and shower floors where grip is the whole point.

What to bring when you visit

  • Room sizes, and which way the long wall runs.
  • Photographs of the space in daylight and under its evening lighting.
  • Any joinery, stone or paint sample already decided on.
  • Your fitting date — lead times on imported ranges are real.

We keep tile, slab and mosaic displays at all three stores, and we will pull the cartons out so you can see the body, the edge and the back, not just the face. If you tell us the room, we will tell you what belongs in it — including when the cheaper tile is the right answer.
